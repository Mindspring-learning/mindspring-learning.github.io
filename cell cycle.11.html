1. Why do cells divide?

All organisms begin life from a single cell. Repeated cycles of growth and division can produce organisms containing millions of cells.

According to cell theory, cells arise from pre-existing cells. For successful division, a cell must coordinate cell growth, DNA replication and cell division so that daughter cells receive intact genomes.

Growth

Cell division increases cell number in multicellular organisms.

Repair and replacement

Cells lost through injury or cell death can be replaced. Examples include cells of the gut lining and blood.

Genetic continuity

DNA must be replicated and distributed to daughter cells in a coordinated way.

Sexual reproduction

Meiosis produces haploid cells; fertilisation restores the diploid phase.

Big picture: The cell cycle describes the events between successive divisions. Mitosis generally maintains chromosome number; meiosis reduces it by half.

2. Cell cycle

The cell cycle is the sequence of events through which a cell duplicates its genome, synthesises other cell components and eventually divides into two daughter cells.

Cell growth, measured as an increase in cytoplasm, is continuous. DNA synthesis occurs only during a specific stage called the S phase. The cell cycle has two main phases: interphase and M phase.

Schematic cell-cycle diagram; phase duration varies with organism and cell type.
NCERT facts: A typical human cell in culture divides approximately every 24 hours. Yeast can complete a cycle in about 90 minutes. In the 24-hour example, interphase occupies more than 95% of the cycle; cell division proper lasts about an hour.

3. Interphase: G₁, S and G₂

Interphase is sometimes called the “resting phase”, but the cell is not inactive. It grows, carries out metabolism and prepares for division.

PhaseEventsKey point
G₁ — Gap 1Cell is metabolically active and grows; much organelle duplication occurs.DNA has not yet replicated.
S — SynthesisDNA replication occurs. In animal cells, centrioles duplicate in the cytoplasm.DNA amount doubles from 2C to 4C; chromosome number remains 2n.
G₂ — Gap 2Cell growth continues and proteins are synthesised in preparation for mitosis.DNA has already replicated.
Mnemonic: “Grow — Synthesize — Gear up”
G₁ = Grow · S = Synthesize DNA · G₂ = Gear up for division.
Important: DNA content doubles during S phase, but chromosome number does not. Each replicated chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at a centromere.

Here, n represents the haploid chromosome-set number; C represents DNA amount relative to the unreplicated diploid starting cell.

G₀ — quiescent stage

Some cells leave G₁ and enter G₀. They remain metabolically active but do not proliferate unless called upon to do so. NCERT mentions heart cells as an example of cells that do not appear to divide, while many other cells divide only occasionally.

G₀: “Goes out, still alive.” It does not mean the cell is dead or metabolically inactive.

4. M phase and mitosis

The M phase is the most dramatic part of the cycle. It begins with karyokinesis (nuclear division) and usually ends with cytokinesis (division of cytoplasm). Mitosis is called equational division because the chromosome number of parent and daughter cells remains the same.

Simplified schematic diagrams of the four stages of mitotic karyokinesis.
Mnemonic: “Please Make All Two”
Prophase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase. Think: prepare, middle, apart, two nuclei.

4.1 Prophase — chromosomes condense

  • Chromatin condenses into compact, visible mitotic chromosomes.
  • Each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids attached at the centromere.
  • Duplicated centrosomes begin moving towards opposite poles.
  • Each centrosome radiates microtubules called asters. The two asters together with spindle fibres form the mitotic apparatus.
  • By the end of prophase, the nucleolus, Golgi complexes, endoplasmic reticulum and nuclear envelope are no longer visible as separate structures.

4.2 Metaphase — chromosomes align

  • Complete disintegration of the nuclear envelope marks the start of metaphase.
  • Chromosome condensation is complete, making chromosome morphology easiest to study under a microscope.
  • Kinetochores are small disc-shaped structures on the centromeres and serve as spindle-fibre attachment sites.
  • Chromosomes align at the cell equator, called the metaphase plate. Sister chromatids connect to spindle fibres from opposite poles.
Remember: kinetochore = attachment site; metaphase plate = alignment plane.

4.3 Anaphase — chromatids separate

  • Centromeres split simultaneously.
  • Sister chromatids separate and are now called daughter chromosomes.
  • They move towards opposite poles, with each centromere directed towards its pole.

4.4 Telophase — nuclei reform

  • Chromosomes reach opposite poles and decondense, losing their distinct compact appearance.
  • Nuclear envelopes develop around the chromosome clusters, forming two daughter nuclei.
  • The nucleolus, Golgi complex and endoplasmic reticulum reform.
Four-stage recap: Prophase = condense · Metaphase = align · Anaphase = separate · Telophase = reform nuclei.

5. Cytokinesis

Karyokinesis divides the nucleus. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm and completes cell division in the usual case.

Animal cell

A cleavage furrow appears in the plasma membrane, deepens inward and eventually divides the cytoplasm into two daughter cells.

Plant cell

A cell plate begins forming in the centre and grows outward until it meets the lateral walls. It develops into the new separating wall.

At the time of cytoplasmic division, organelles such as mitochondria and plastids are distributed between daughter cells.

NCERT example — syncytium: If karyokinesis is not followed by cytokinesis, a multinucleate condition called a syncytium may form. Example: liquid endosperm of coconut.

6. Significance of mitosis

  • Growth: increases the number of cells in multicellular organisms.
  • Genetic continuity: usually produces daughter cells with an identical genetic complement and the same chromosome number.
  • Repair and replacement: cells of the upper epidermis, gut lining and blood are continually replaced.
  • Plant growth: mitotic divisions in meristematic tissues, including apical meristems and lateral cambium, support continuous growth throughout plant life.
  • Nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio: division helps restore the ratio disturbed as the cell grows.
Mitosis = G-R-R-C
Growth · Repair · Replacement · Conservation of chromosome number.

7. Meiosis — reduction division

Sexual reproduction involves fusion of two haploid gametes. Meiosis is a specialised division in diploid cells that reduces chromosome number by half and produces haploid daughter cells. Fertilisation restores the diploid phase.

One DNA replication

DNA replicates once before meiosis I.

Two divisions

Meiosis consists of meiosis I and meiosis II.

Pairing and recombination

Homologous chromosomes pair, and recombination occurs between non-sister chromatids.

Four haploid cells

Four haploid daughter cells form at the end of meiosis II.

Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes; meiosis II separates sister chromatids.
Meiosis: “I separate Homologues; II separate Sisters.”
Meiosis I is reductional division. Meiosis II resembles mitosis in the separation of sister chromatids.

8. Prophase I: five sub-stages

Prophase I is longer and more complex than mitotic prophase. The five stages are identified by chromosome behaviour.

StageEventsMemory cue
LeptoteneChromosomes gradually become visible under the light microscope; compaction continues.Look
ZygoteneHomologous chromosomes begin pairing. This is synapsis. A synaptonemal complex forms; the paired unit is a bivalent.Zip together
PachyteneFour chromatids of each bivalent are distinct as a tetrad. Crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids at recombination nodules. The enzyme involved is recombinase.Pair and swap
DiploteneThe synaptonemal complex dissolves; homologues begin separating but remain connected at crossover sites called chiasmata. In some vertebrate oocytes this stage can last months or years.Drift apart, stay linked
DiakinesisChiasmata terminalise; chromosomes become fully condensed; spindle assembles; nucleolus disappears and nuclear envelope breaks down.Disassemble nucleus
Mnemonic: “Lazy Zebras Prefer Delicious Dates”
Leptotene → Zygotene → Pachytene → Diplotene → Diakinesis.
Conceptual schematic; the exchange occurs between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.
Do not mix up these terms:
Synapsis = pairing of homologous chromosomes.
Crossing over = exchange of genetic material between non-sister chromatids.
Chiasmata = visible X-shaped connections at crossover sites as homologues separate.

9. Meiosis I and meiosis II

Meiosis I — reductional division

  1. Prophase I: homologous chromosomes pair and recombination occurs.
  2. Metaphase I: bivalents align on the equatorial plate. Spindle microtubules from opposite poles attach to kinetochores of homologous chromosomes.
  3. Anaphase I: homologous chromosomes separate and move to opposite poles. Sister chromatids remain joined at their centromeres. Each pole receives half the parent cell's chromosome number.
  4. Telophase I: nuclear membrane and nucleolus reappear; cytokinesis follows, producing a dyad of cells.
Interkinesis: the generally short-lived stage between meiosis I and II. DNA replication does not occur during interkinesis.

Meiosis II — sister chromatids separate

  1. Prophase II: chromosomes become compact again; the nuclear membrane disappears by the end.
  2. Metaphase II: chromosomes align at the equator; spindle microtubules attach to kinetochores of sister chromatids from opposite poles.
  3. Anaphase II: centromeres split; sister chromatids separate and move towards opposite poles.
  4. Telophase II: nuclear envelopes form around chromosome groups; cytokinesis produces a tetrad of four haploid daughter cells.
“Homologues first, sisters second.”
Anaphase I separates homologous chromosomes; anaphase II separates sister chromatids.

10. Significance of meiosis

  • Maintains chromosome number across generations: meiosis halves chromosome number; fertilisation restores it.
  • Genetic variability: recombination increases genetic variation in populations.
  • Evolution: variation is important for evolution.
Meiosis = “Half + Mix + Maintain”
Half the chromosome number · Mix genetic material through recombination · Maintain the species chromosome number across sexual generations with fertilisation.
