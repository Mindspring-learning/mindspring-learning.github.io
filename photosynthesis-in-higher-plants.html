01 · Learn the concept

What is photosynthesis?

Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae and some bacteria capture light energy and use it to synthesise organic compounds from carbon dioxide and water. In oxygenic photosynthesis, water is the electron donor and oxygen is released.

Overall simplified equation
6 CO₂ + 12 H₂O
+ light →
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ + 6 H₂O

This is a simplified net representation. Oxygen released during oxygenic photosynthesis comes from water, not carbon dioxide.

Big picture: Light reactions convert light energy into ATP and NADPH. The Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to incorporate CO₂ into carbohydrate precursors. The Calvin cycle does not directly require photons, but it depends on products of the light reactions.

Where does it occur?

In higher plants, photosynthesis occurs mainly in the mesophyll cells of leaves. Chloroplasts contain a double-membrane envelope. The internal fluid is the stroma; flattened membrane sacs are thylakoids, often stacked into grana and connected by stroma lamellae.

Grana
Stacks of thylakoids; provide membrane surface for photosystems and electron transport.
Stroma
Fluid matrix containing enzymes for the Calvin cycle, including RuBisCO.
Thylakoid membrane
Contains chlorophyll, photosystems, electron carriers and ATP synthase.
Lumen
Thylakoid interior where protons accumulate during light-driven electron transport.

Photosynthetic pigments

Pigments absorb light of particular wavelengths. In higher plants, chlorophyll a is the chief pigment associated with reaction centres. Chlorophyll b and carotenoids act as accessory pigments, broadening the range of light absorbed and transferring energy to chlorophyll a. Carotenoids also help protect the photosynthetic apparatus from photo-oxidative damage.

Light reaction: what happens?

  1. Pigments absorb photons and transfer excitation energy within the photosystem antenna.
  2. Reaction-centre chlorophyll becomes excited and an electron is transferred to an acceptor.
  3. In PSII, water is oxidised by the oxygen-evolving complex, replacing lost electrons and releasing protons and O₂.
  4. Electrons move through carriers; electron transport contributes to a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane.
  5. Protons flow through chloroplast ATP synthase, supporting ATP formation. PSI re-energises electrons that ultimately reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH.
Remember: PSII acts before PSI in the usual non-cyclic electron-flow pathway. Their names reflect discovery order, not the order in the pathway.

Photolysis of water

Water oxidation supplies electrons to PSII, releases protons into the thylakoid lumen and liberates oxygen. The oxygen released is derived from water molecules.

Chemiosmosis in chloroplasts

ATP synthesis is coupled to a proton electrochemical gradient. Protons accumulate in the thylakoid lumen through water oxidation and electron transport; reduction of NADP⁺ also removes protons from the stroma. The resulting gradient drives protons back to the stroma through ATP synthase, which forms ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate.

Common confusion: Chloroplast chemiosmosis occurs across the thylakoid membrane. Do not confuse it with mitochondrial chemiosmosis across the inner mitochondrial membrane.
02 · Experiments and discoveries

Scientists you should know

Joseph Priestley
Experiments showed that plants could restore air affected by a burning candle or animal respiration.
Jan Ingenhousz
Showed that sunlight and green parts of plants are important for releasing oxygen.
Julius von Sachs
Showed that green parts form glucose, commonly stored as starch, during photosynthesis.
T. W. Engelmann
Used a prism and aerobic bacteria to show that blue and red light are especially effective in photosynthesis.
C. B. van Niel
Proposed a general equation and helped establish that oxygen in oxygenic photosynthesis comes from water.
Melvin Calvin and colleagues
Used radioactive carbon-14 to trace carbon fixation and elucidate the Calvin cycle.
Exam link: Engelmann's experiment helped relate the action spectrum of photosynthesis to wavelengths absorbed effectively by photosynthetic pigments.

04 · Interactive cycle

Calvin cycle: follow the carbon

The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma. It uses ATP and NADPH made by the light reactions to reduce CO₂ and regenerate the CO₂ acceptor, RuBP.

1. FixationCO₂ + RuBP
2. Reduction3-PGA → G3P
3. RegenerationRuBP restored
PHASE 1 OF 3
Carbon fixation

For every 3 CO₂ entering the cycle, 3 molecules of RuBP (each with 5 carbons) are acted on by RuBisCO. The unstable 6-carbon intermediates split, yielding 6 molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA).

05 · Pathway comparison

C₃ and C₄ plants

In C₃ plants, the first stable product of CO₂ fixation in the Calvin cycle is 3-PGA (a 3-carbon compound). C₄ plants first fix CO₂ into a 4-carbon compound in mesophyll cells, then release concentrated CO₂ in bundle-sheath cells for the Calvin cycle.

06 · High-yield concept

Photorespiration

RuBisCO can act as both a carboxylase and an oxygenase. When it reacts with O₂ instead of CO₂, oxygenation of RuBP initiates photorespiration. This pathway consumes energy and releases previously fixed CO₂, without producing sugar; it is therefore often described as wasteful from the plant's carbon-gain perspective.

  • More likely when the O₂:CO₂ ratio around RuBisCO rises, such as in hot, dry conditions when stomata close and internal CO₂ falls.
  • Involves chloroplasts, peroxisomes and mitochondria.
  • C₄ plants reduce photorespiration by concentrating CO₂ in bundle-sheath cells.
Trap alert: Photorespiration is not the same as mitochondrial cellular respiration. It is linked to RuBisCO's oxygenase activity and does not yield ATP for the plant.

08 · Limiting factors

Factors affecting photosynthesis

  • Light: Rate often increases with light intensity until another factor becomes limiting; excessive light can cause photoinhibition.
  • CO₂ concentration: Raising CO₂ can increase the rate up to a saturation point, depending on other conditions.
  • Temperature: Enzyme-controlled reactions have a temperature range and optimum; extreme heat can reduce the rate.
  • Water: Water is a reactant. Water stress can also cause stomatal closure, reducing CO₂ entry.

Blackman's law of limiting factors: When a process depends on several factors, its rate is limited by the factor that is nearest its minimum relative to the requirement, while other factors are adequate.

Interactive idea: Change one factor at a time when investigating photosynthesis; if two factors change simultaneously, it becomes difficult to identify the cause of a rate change.

Mnemonics and memory hooks

Calvin cycle: “Fix – Reduce – Regenerate”
Fix CO₂ onto RuBP; reduce 3-PGA using ATP/NADPH; regenerate RuBP so the cycle continues.
C₄ cells: “M first, B next”
Mesophyll: initial CO₂ fixation by PEP carboxylase. Bundle sheath: CO₂ release and Calvin cycle.
Photosystems: “II then I”
Non-cyclic electron flow reaches PSII first, then PSI, despite the numbering.

13 · Before the test

Last-minute revision sheet

  • Chlorophyll a is the chief pigment; accessory pigments broaden light absorption and provide photoprotection.
  • Light reactions occur in the thylakoid membrane; the Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma.
  • PSII → electron transport → PSI is the usual non-cyclic flow; water oxidation at PSII releases O₂.
  • Non-cyclic flow forms ATP and NADPH and releases O₂; cyclic flow around PSI increases ATP production without directly producing NADPH or O₂.
  • Chloroplast ATP synthase uses a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane.
  • Calvin cycle phases: fixation, reduction and regeneration.
  • For 3 CO₂: 9 ATP and 6 NADPH are used, with one net G3P produced.
  • C₄ plants initially fix CO₂ with PEP carboxylase; the Calvin cycle occurs in bundle-sheath cells in typical Kranz anatomy.
  • Photorespiration begins with RuBisCO oxygenase activity and reduces net carbon gain.
  • Light, CO₂, temperature and water can limit the photosynthetic rate.
