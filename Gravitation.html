01 · The big picture

What you should understand by the end of this chapter.

Core idea

Universal gravitation

Every object with mass attracts every other object with mass. The force acts along the line joining their centres and is always attractive in the Newtonian model.

Earth and beyond

Gravity controls motion

The same gravitational interaction explains falling bodies, the Moon's orbit, planetary motion, and artificial satellites.

Important distinction

Mass is not weight

Mass measures inertia and is measured in kilograms. Weight is the gravitational force on a body; near Earth's surface, its magnitude is approximately mg.

02 · Newton's universal law of gravitation

Two masses attract each other with a force proportional to their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.

Mathematical form

F = G m₁m₂ / r²F: gravitational force (N) · m₁, m₂: masses (kg) · r: centre-to-centre distance (m)

Doubling either mass doubles the force. Doubling the separation makes the force one-fourth as large.

The distance r is measured between the centres of the two bodies, not between their nearest surfaces.

Universal gravitational constant

G ≈ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²

G is universal: its value does not depend on which planet or object is being considered.

Gravitational forces between everyday objects are usually tiny because G is very small.

Worked example · Inverse-square reasoning

If the distance between two masses becomes three times the original distance while the masses stay unchanged:

F′ = Gm₁m₂/(3r)² = F/9

The new force is one-ninth of the original force. Always square the distance factor.

03 · Acceleration due to gravity

Why the value of g changes with altitude, depth, and the planet you are on.

At a planet's surface

g = GM / R²M: mass of the planet · R: radius of the planet

For Earth, the approximate surface value is 9.8 m s⁻². It depends on the planet's mass and radius.

Mass vs weight

W = mgWeight W is a force, measured in newtons (N).

A person's mass remains approximately the same when travelling between planets, but their weight changes with the local gravitational acceleration.

At height h above the surface

gₕ = GM / (R + h)²Equivalently: gₕ = g [R/(R+h)]²

As altitude increases, the distance from Earth's centre increases, so gravitational acceleration decreases.

At depth d below the surface

gᵈ ≈ g (1 − d/R)For a uniform-density Earth model.

In the standard NCERT model, g decreases linearly with depth and becomes zero at Earth's centre. This result assumes uniform density.

Exam caution: The simple depth relation assumes a uniform-density Earth. Real Earth has varying density, so treat this as the textbook model rather than an exact geophysical description.

04 · Gravitational potential and potential energy

Use these ideas to describe the energy of a mass in a gravitational field.

Gravitational potential energy

U = −GMm/rFor two masses with zero potential energy chosen at infinite separation.

The negative sign reflects that gravity is attractive and the bound system has less energy than the separated masses at infinity.

Gravitational potential

V = U/m = −GM/rPotential is potential energy per unit test mass; SI unit: J kg⁻¹.

Potential is a scalar. Potential energy depends on the mass placed in the field, while potential at a point describes the field source.

Near Earth's surface

ΔU ≈ mgΔh

For small height changes near the surface, gravitational potential energy changes approximately by mass × g × height change.

Potential and field direction

For a spherically symmetric mass, gravitational potential becomes less negative as distance from the mass increases, approaching zero at infinity under this convention.

Gravitational force is attractive; gravitational potential energy for a bound pair is negative with the usual zero-at-infinity reference.

05 · Satellites and orbital motion

A satellite is continually falling towards Earth while moving sideways fast enough to keep missing the surface.

Orbital speed for a circular orbit

vₒ = √(GM/r)r is measured from the centre of the planet.

For a circular orbit, gravity provides the required centripetal force. At a larger orbital radius, the circular orbital speed is lower.

Time period

T = 2π √(r³/GM)For a circular orbit around a central mass M.

A satellite farther away takes longer to complete one orbit. This is consistent with Kepler's third law: T² is proportional to r³ for a fixed central mass.

Escape speed

vₑ = √(2GM/R) = √(2gR)Ideal minimum launch speed from the surface, neglecting atmosphere and Earth's rotation.

Escape speed is the speed needed to reach infinity with zero residual speed in the ideal energy model. It does not depend on the mass of the escaping object.

Energy of a circular-orbit satellite

K = +GMm/(2r)
U = −GMm/r
E = −GMm/(2r)

The total mechanical energy is negative for a bound circular orbit. K is positive, U is negative, and E = K + U.

Geostationary satellites · Key features

  • Orbit in the equatorial plane.
  • Move in the same direction as Earth's rotation.
  • Have an orbital period equal to one sidereal day (about 23 h 56 min).
  • Appear approximately fixed above one point on Earth's equator, making them useful for communications and weather monitoring.

A satellite merely having a 24-hour period is not enough; the orbital plane and direction must also be correct.

06 · Kepler's laws of planetary motion

Three observations that Newton's law of gravitation helps explain.

First law

Law of orbits

Planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus.

Second law

Law of areas

The line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time. A planet moves faster when nearer the Sun.

Third law

Law of periods

T² ∝ a³

The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis for bodies orbiting the same central mass.

Why these laws matter

They connect the geometry and timing of planetary orbits. Newton's gravitational law provides a physical explanation for these regularities.

09 · Common exam traps

Small checks that prevent avoidable mistakes.

01 · Distance means centre-to-centre

For separated spherical bodies, do not substitute the gap between their surfaces for r.

02 · Do not forget the square

If distance becomes n times, force becomes 1/n² times when masses are unchanged.

03 · G and g are different

G is universal; g is local gravitational acceleration and changes with the planet and location.

04 · Escape speed vs orbital speed

At the same radius in the ideal model, escape speed is √2 times circular orbital speed.

05 · Potential energy has a sign

With zero at infinity, gravitational potential energy is negative for a bound pair.

06 · Use radius from the centre

For a satellite at altitude h, use r = R + h in orbital formulas.
