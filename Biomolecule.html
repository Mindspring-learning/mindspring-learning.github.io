01 · Quick Revision Notes

What are biomolecules?

Biomolecules are chemical compounds found in living organisms. They help build cell structures and carry out essential life processes.

Major groups

  • Carbohydrates: Important energy sources and structural materials.
  • Proteins: Made of amino acids; many act as enzymes.
  • Lipids: Include fats, oils and phospholipids; important in energy storage and membranes.
  • Nucleic acids: DNA and RNA; involved in storing and expressing genetic information.
Remember: Enzymes are biological catalysts. Most enzymes are proteins, although some RNA molecules can also catalyse reactions.

Carbohydrates

  • Monosaccharides: Simple sugars, such as glucose and fructose.
  • Disaccharides: Two monosaccharide units, such as sucrose and lactose.
  • Polysaccharides: Long chains of sugar units, such as starch, glycogen and cellulose.

Proteins

Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins. Amino acids are joined by peptide bonds to form polypeptide chains. A protein's shape is closely linked to its function.

Lipids

Many fats contain glycerol and fatty acids. Phospholipids are important components of cell membranes. Lipids are grouped with biomolecules but are not true polymers made of repeating monomer units like proteins.

Nucleic acids

Nucleotides are the building blocks of DNA and RNA. Each nucleotide contains a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar and a phosphate group.

Chemical Analysis of Living Tissues

1. Elemental analysis

Living matter contains both organic and inorganic elements. Carbon is a major element in organic molecules. Hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus and sulphur are also important components of many biomolecules.

Elements are the basic chemical building blocks; they combine in different ways to form molecules with different properties and functions.

3. Compound analysis

In compound analysis, a tissue sample is processed so that its chemical constituents can be examined. The compounds identified may include amino acids, sugars, nitrogen-containing bases and organic acids.

Some molecules are small and simple, while others are large molecules built from smaller units.

4. Primary and secondary metabolites

Primary metabolites are compounds directly associated with normal growth, development and maintenance. Examples include amino acids, sugars and many organic acids.

Secondary metabolites are compounds that are not directly involved in the basic processes of growth and reproduction in the same way. Many have ecological roles, such as defence or attracting pollinators.

  • Examples of secondary metabolites: alkaloids, pigments, essential oils, toxins, lectins, drugs, rubber and gums.
  • Important note: secondary metabolites can still be biologically important; “secondary” does not mean useless.

02 · Chemical Analysis of Living Tissues

All living organisms are made up of chemical substances called biomolecules. Studying their chemical composition helps us understand how cells are organised and how living systems function.

1. Elements in living and non-living matter

When scientists analyse plant tissue, animal tissue or microbial cells, they find elements such as carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. Many of these elements are also present in the Earth's crust.

The important difference is their relative abundance. Living matter generally has a higher proportion of carbon and hydrogen relative to many other elements than the Earth's crust.

Remember: Living and non-living matter can contain the same elements, but the relative proportions may differ.

2. How are chemical constituents analysed?

Scientists can study a tissue sample by grinding it in a suitable chemical solution and separating the resulting mixture into fractions. In the NCERT method, trichloroacetic acid is used to prepare a tissue extract.

  • Acid-soluble pool: The filtrate contains many small molecules, including amino acids, sugars and other low-molecular-weight compounds.
  • Acid-insoluble fraction: The material retained after filtration contains large cellular structures and macromolecular components, including proteins, nucleic acids and polysaccharides.

These fractions help scientists separate and study the different constituents of living tissue.

3. Wet weight, dry weight and ash

  • Wet weight: The mass of a fresh tissue sample, including its water.
  • Dry weight: The mass remaining after the water has been removed by drying.
  • Ash: The inorganic residue left after the dried tissue is completely burnt under appropriate conditions.

During burning, most organic compounds are converted into gaseous products. The remaining ash contains inorganic constituents, such as mineral elements and their compounds.

Exam distinction: Drying removes water; complete burning removes most organic matter and leaves inorganic residue.

4. Elemental analysis vs compound analysis

Identifies the elements present. Identifies the compounds present.
Examples: carbon, oxygen, nitrogen. Examples: amino acids, sugars, fatty acids.

5. Biomolecules and metabolites

Chemical compounds found in living organisms are called biomolecules. Many of the small organic compounds involved in cellular reactions are called metabolites.

From a biological point of view, compounds can be classified into groups such as amino acids, sugars, fatty acids and nitrogenous bases. Their structures determine their properties and functions.

6. Alpha-amino acids

Amino acids are important building blocks of proteins. A typical alpha-amino acid has an amino group (–NH₂) and a carboxyl group (–COOH) attached to the same central carbon, called the alpha-carbon.

The alpha-carbon is also attached to:

  • A hydrogen atom (–H)
  • A variable side chain called the R group

The R group differs among amino acids and influences their properties. The usual textbook representation is:

7. Primary and secondary metabolites

Primary metabolites have identifiable roles in normal physiological and metabolic processes. Examples include amino acids, sugars and many organic acids.

Secondary metabolites are compounds commonly found in plants, fungi and microorganisms that are not directly involved in the same basic metabolic roles. Many have specialised ecological functions or are useful to humans.

NCERT examples of secondary metabolites include:

  • Alkaloids
  • Flavonoids
  • Rubber
  • Essential oils
  • Antibiotics
  • Coloured pigments and scents
  • Gums and spices
  • Lectins, such as Concanavalin A

Their roles are diverse. Some protect organisms or influence interactions with other organisms; others are useful as medicines, flavouring agents, pigments or industrial materials.

Important: We do not understand the biological role of every secondary metabolite completely. Secondary does not mean unimportant.

8. Quick revision — must-know points

  • Living tissue contains both organic and inorganic constituents.
  • Elemental analysis identifies elements; compound analysis identifies compounds.
  • Wet weight includes water; dry weight is measured after water removal.
  • Ash is the inorganic residue left after complete burning.
  • The acid-soluble pool is rich in small molecules.
  • The acid-insoluble fraction contains macromolecular components.
  • An alpha-amino acid has amino and carboxyl groups attached to its alpha-carbon.
  • Primary metabolites participate in normal physiological processes.
  • Secondary metabolites include alkaloids, flavonoids, rubber and essential oils.

03 · Biomacromolecules

A living cell contains thousands of chemical compounds. Some are small molecules, while others are large and complex. These substances take part in cellular structure, energy transfer, growth, repair and the chemical reactions essential for life.

1. What are biomolecules?

Biomolecules are chemical compounds found in living organisms. They include carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, amino acids, sugars, nucleotides and many other substances.

Biomolecules differ in size, structure and function. For example, glucose is a relatively small molecule used in energy metabolism, whereas DNA is a large molecule that stores hereditary information.

2. Micromolecules and macromolecules

Biomolecules can be grouped broadly according to their molecular size and behaviour during chemical analysis.

  • Micromolecules: Relatively small molecules, commonly found in the acid-soluble pool. Examples include amino acids, simple sugars and nucleotides.
  • Macromolecules: Large molecules generally found in the acid-insoluble fraction. Examples include proteins, nucleic acids and polysaccharides.

Many biological macromolecules have molecular masses in the thousands or much higher. Their large size allows them to form complex structures and perform specialised functions.

Important distinction: Lipids are generally found in the acid-insoluble fraction, but most individual lipid molecules are not true macromolecules in the same sense as proteins, nucleic acids and polysaccharides. Lipids often occur as components of membranes and other large cellular assemblies.

Acid-soluble pool: Contains many small organic compounds, such as amino acids, sugars, nucleotides and organic acids. Inorganic ions may also be present.

Acid-insoluble fraction: Contains cellular structures and large molecular components, including proteins, nucleic acids and polysaccharides. Lipids are also recovered with this fraction during the extraction process.

The distinction is based on the behaviour of substances during extraction and separation. It is not simply a list of molecules classified by size alone.

4. The four major classes of biomolecules

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates include sugars and their larger forms. They provide energy, store energy and perform structural functions. Examples include glucose, sucrose, starch and cellulose.

Proteins

Proteins are chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. They function as enzymes, structural materials, transporters, receptors and many other essential cellular components.

Lipids

Lipids include fats, oils, phospholipids and steroids. Many lipids are hydrophobic, meaning they do not mix readily with water. They store energy, form important membrane components and participate in signalling.

Nucleic acids

DNA and RNA are nucleic acids made from nucleotide units. DNA stores hereditary information, while RNA has several roles in gene expression and protein synthesis.

5. What are monomers and polymers?

A monomer is a small molecular unit that can be joined to other units. A polymer is a large molecule made from repeating or linked smaller units.

  • Amino acids form proteins.
  • Monosaccharides can form polysaccharides.
  • Nucleotides form nucleic acids.

However, not every large biological molecule is a polymer. Lipids such as triglycerides are not constructed as long chains of repeating monomer units in the same way as proteins or DNA.

6. Why is water important?

Water forms a major part of living cells. It provides a medium for many biochemical reactions, helps transport dissolved substances and influences the structure and behaviour of biomolecules.

Many small polar compounds dissolve in water, while hydrophobic substances such as many lipids have limited solubility. This difference helps cells organise membranes and other structures.

8. Exam essentials

  • Proteins, nucleic acids and polysaccharides are biological macromolecules.
  • The acid-soluble pool contains many small molecules.
  • The acid-insoluble fraction contains macromolecules and cellular structures.
  • Lipids occur in the acid-insoluble fraction but are not generally true macromolecules.
  • Proteins are made of amino acids; nucleic acids are made of nucleotides.
  • DNA and RNA are nucleic acids, not proteins.
  • Not all biomolecules are polymers.

04 · Carbohydrates and Polysaccharides

Carbohydrates are organic compounds that commonly contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. They are important as energy sources, energy stores and structural materials in living organisms.

1. Classification of carbohydrates

  • Monosaccharides: Simple sugars that cannot be broken down into smaller carbohydrate units by hydrolysis. Examples: glucose, fructose and ribose.
  • Oligosaccharides: Carbohydrates containing a small number of monosaccharide units. Disaccharides such as sucrose, maltose and lactose belong here.
  • Polysaccharides: Large carbohydrate molecules made from many monosaccharide units. Examples: starch, glycogen and cellulose.

2. Monosaccharides

Glucose is a six-carbon sugar and an important molecule in cellular energy metabolism. Fructose is another six-carbon sugar. Ribose is a five-carbon sugar found in RNA, while deoxyribose occurs in DNA.

Monosaccharides may be classified according to the number of carbon atoms and the type of carbonyl group they contain.

  • Aldoses: Contain an aldehyde group in their open-chain form; glucose is an example.
  • Ketoses: Contain a ketone group in their open-chain form; fructose is an example.
  • Pentoses: Have five carbon atoms; ribose is an example.
  • Hexoses: Have six carbon atoms; glucose and fructose are examples.

3. Disaccharides

A disaccharide contains two monosaccharide units joined by a glycosidic bond. Hydrolysis breaks this bond and releases smaller sugar units.

4. Polysaccharides

Polysaccharides are long carbohydrate chains containing many monosaccharide units. Their properties depend on the sugar units, the glycosidic linkages and the arrangement of the chains.

Starch

Starch is a major storage carbohydrate in plants. It consists of glucose units and contains two main components:

  • Amylose: Mostly unbranched chains with alpha-1,4 linkages.
  • Amylopectin: Branched chains with alpha-1,4 linkages along the chains and alpha-1,6 linkages at branch points.

Glycogen

Glycogen is a storage polysaccharide in animals and many fungi. It resembles amylopectin but is more extensively branched. In animals, it is stored especially in the liver and muscles.

Cellulose

Cellulose is an important structural component of plant cell walls. It consists of glucose units joined by beta-1,4 glycosidic linkages. Its chains form strong fibres. Humans lack the enzyme cellulase needed to digest cellulose efficiently.

Chitin

Chitin is a structural polysaccharide found in arthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls. Its repeating units are modified sugars containing nitrogen.

05 · Proteins and Their Structure

Proteins are nitrogen-containing biological macromolecules made from amino acids. They perform a wide range of functions, including catalysis, transport, defence, movement and structural support.

1. Amino acids: the building blocks

A typical alpha-amino acid has an alpha-carbon attached to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom and a variable R group. The R group determines many of its chemical properties.

Amino acids differ in their side chains. Some are non-polar, some are polar, and some carry charged side chains under particular conditions.

2. Peptide bonds

A peptide bond forms between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another. Formation of the bond involves removal of the elements of water in a condensation reaction.

A short chain of amino acids is called a peptide. A longer chain is a polypeptide. One or more polypeptide chains can fold or assemble to form a functional protein.

3. Four levels of protein structure

Primary structure

The primary structure is the specific linear sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide. Changing the sequence can alter folding and biological activity.

Secondary structure

The polypeptide backbone forms regular local patterns, including the alpha-helix and beta-pleated sheet. Hydrogen bonding helps stabilise these structures.

Tertiary structure

Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional folding of a single polypeptide chain. It is influenced by interactions between side chains, including hydrophobic interactions, ionic interactions, hydrogen bonds and disulphide bonds.

Quaternary structure

Quaternary structure occurs when multiple polypeptide subunits associate to form a functional protein. Haemoglobin is a familiar example with four subunits.

4. Fibrous and globular proteins

  • Fibrous proteins: Often elongated and structural. Examples include collagen and keratin.
  • Globular proteins: Usually compact and involved in functions such as catalysis, transport or regulation. Examples include many enzymes and haemoglobin.

5. Denaturation

Heat, extreme pH and certain chemicals can disrupt the interactions that maintain a protein's shape. This is called denaturation. The protein may lose its biological activity.

Denaturation generally disrupts higher levels of structure rather than breaking the peptide bonds of the primary sequence. The precise effect depends on the protein and the conditions.

6. Functions of proteins

  • Enzymatic: Catalyse biochemical reactions.
  • Structural: Collagen and keratin provide strength and support.
  • Transport: Haemoglobin carries oxygen in blood.
  • Defence: Antibodies participate in immune defence.
  • Movement: Actin and myosin participate in muscle contraction.
  • Regulation: Some hormones are proteins or peptides.

7. Exam essentials

  • Amino acids are linked by peptide bonds.
  • The amino-acid sequence determines the primary structure.
  • Alpha-helices and beta-sheets are examples of secondary structure.
  • Tertiary structure describes the three-dimensional folding of a chain.
  • Quaternary structure involves multiple polypeptide subunits.
  • Denaturation usually affects shape and function without breaking the primary sequence.

06 · Lipids

Lipids are a diverse group of biological substances that are generally poorly soluble in water. They include fats, oils, phospholipids, waxes and steroids.

1. Fatty acids

A fatty acid contains a carboxyl group attached to a hydrocarbon chain. Fatty acids differ in chain length and in the number and arrangement of carbon-carbon double bonds.

  • Saturated fatty acids: Have no carbon-carbon double bonds in their hydrocarbon chain.
  • Unsaturated fatty acids: Have one or more carbon-carbon double bonds.

2. Triglycerides

A triglyceride consists of one glycerol molecule linked to three fatty acids by ester bonds. Formation of these bonds involves condensation reactions.

4. Steroids and waxes

  • Steroids: Have a characteristic four-ring carbon framework. Cholesterol is a component of animal cell membranes and a precursor of steroid hormones.
  • Waxes: Hydrophobic substances that help reduce water loss or provide protective coatings in organisms.

5. Why are lipids in the acid-insoluble fraction?

In tissue extraction, lipids are generally recovered with the acid-insoluble fraction because many occur in membranes and other structures. However, most individual lipid molecules have much lower molecular masses than true biological macromolecules such as proteins and nucleic acids.

6. Exam essentials

  • Fatty acids contain a carboxyl group and a hydrocarbon chain.
  • Triglycerides contain glycerol and three fatty acids.
  • Ester bonds link fatty acids to glycerol in triglycerides.
  • Phospholipids have hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails.
  • Phospholipids form bilayers in biological membranes.
  • Cholesterol is a steroid.
  • Lipids are not generally polymers made of repeating monomer units.

07 · Nucleic Acids: DNA and RNA

Nucleic acids are biological macromolecules made of nucleotide units. DNA and RNA are essential for storing, transmitting and using genetic information.

1. What is a nucleotide?

A nucleotide consists of three components:

  • A nitrogenous base
  • A pentose sugar
  • One or more phosphate groups

A nucleoside contains a nitrogenous base attached to a sugar, but does not contain a phosphate group. Adding phosphate produces a nucleotide.

2. Nitrogenous bases

The main bases in nucleic acids belong to two groups:

  • Purines: Adenine (A) and guanine (G).
  • Pyrimidines: Cytosine (C), thymine (T) and uracil (U).

DNA uses A, G, C and T. RNA uses A, G, C and U. Thus, uracil is found in RNA in place of thymine in the usual comparison of these two nucleic acids.

3. DNA and RNA sugars

  • DNA: Contains deoxyribose sugar.
  • RNA: Contains ribose sugar.

Deoxyribose has one less oxygen atom than ribose at the corresponding carbon position. The sugar and phosphate groups contribute to the backbone of a nucleic acid chain.

4. Phosphodiester bonds

Nucleotides in a nucleic acid chain are joined through phosphodiester bonds. These link the sugar of one nucleotide to the phosphate associated with the next nucleotide.

The resulting strand has directionality, commonly described using its 5′ and 3′ ends.

5. DNA structure

DNA commonly consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands arranged in a double helix. Their nitrogenous bases pair specifically:

  • Adenine pairs with thymine (A–T).
  • Guanine pairs with cytosine (G–C).

Hydrogen bonds help stabilise the paired bases. The sugar-phosphate backbones lie on the outside of the double helix, while the paired bases lie toward the inside.

6. RNA structure and functions

RNA is usually single-stranded, although it can fold into complex shapes through internal base pairing.

  • mRNA (messenger RNA): Carries information used to specify a protein sequence.
  • tRNA (transfer RNA): Helps deliver amino acids during protein synthesis.
  • rRNA (ribosomal RNA): Forms part of ribosomes and contributes to their function.

  • A nucleotide contains a base, a pentose sugar and phosphate.
  • A nucleoside contains a base and sugar, without phosphate.
  • A and G are purines; C, T and U are pyrimidines.
  • DNA contains deoxyribose and thymine.

08 · Metabolism and the Dynamic State of Life

Living cells are not chemically static. Molecules are continually synthesised, modified and broken down. The totality of these chemical reactions is called metabolism.

1. Anabolism and catabolism

  • Anabolism: Reactions that build complex molecules from simpler substances. Biosynthesis of proteins from amino acids is an example.
  • Catabolism: Reactions that break down molecules into simpler products. The breakdown of glucose during cellular respiration is an example.

Anabolic reactions often require an input of energy, whereas catabolic reactions often release energy that cells can capture. The actual energy change depends on the reaction and its conditions.

2. Metabolic pathways

A metabolic pathway is a series of linked chemical reactions. The product of one reaction may become the substrate of the next. Different enzymes usually catalyse the different steps.

Pathways can be linear, branched or cyclic. Cells regulate them so that the production and use of substances match their needs.

3. Why is metabolism essential?

  • It provides energy for cellular activities.
  • It produces building materials for growth and repair.
  • It allows cells to synthesise essential molecules.
  • It helps maintain internal organisation.
  • It supports responses to changes in the environment.

4. Metabolism and living systems

Many individual chemical reactions can also occur outside living cells under suitable conditions. However, a living system is distinguished by the coordinated, regulated network of reactions taking place within an organised cellular system.

Enzymes make these reactions proceed rapidly enough for life and help control their rates.

5. Exam essentials

  • Metabolism is the sum of chemical reactions in an organism.
  • Anabolism builds complex molecules.
  • Catabolism breaks down molecules.
  • Metabolic pathways consist of linked reactions.
  • Enzymes catalyse most biochemical reactions.
  • Living systems maintain a dynamic, regulated chemical state.

09 · Enzymes: Biological Catalysts

Enzymes are biological catalysts that increase the rate of biochemical reactions without being consumed as reactants. Most enzymes are proteins, although some RNA molecules also possess catalytic activity.

1. What is a catalyst?

A catalyst increases the rate of a chemical reaction by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation-energy barrier. It does not change the overall free-energy difference between reactants and products or the reaction's equilibrium position.

2. Substrate and active site

The molecule on which an enzyme acts is called its substrate. A region of the enzyme called the active site binds the substrate and helps the reaction occur.

        |
        v
Enzyme-substrate complex
        |
        v
Enzyme + Product(s)

The enzyme can participate in another catalytic cycle after the products are released.

The shape and chemical properties of the active site contribute to substrate recognition and catalysis. The induced-fit model describes how binding can involve changes in enzyme shape.

3. Activation energy

Reactant molecules must overcome an energy barrier before they can form products. This barrier is called activation energy. Enzymes lower the barrier for the reaction pathway, increasing the reaction rate under suitable conditions.

4. Factors affecting enzyme activity

Temperature

Enzyme activity generally rises with temperature up to an optimum range because molecular collisions become more frequent. Above the suitable range, the enzyme's structure may be disrupted, reducing activity.

pH

Every enzyme has a pH range in which it functions effectively. Changes in pH can alter charges at the active site or affect the enzyme's structure. Different enzymes have different pH optima.

Substrate concentration

As substrate concentration rises, reaction rate usually increases until most available active sites are occupied. Beyond this point, the rate approaches a maximum under those conditions.

Enzyme concentration

If sufficient substrate is available, increasing the amount of active enzyme can increase the reaction rate.

Inhibitors

Inhibitors reduce enzyme activity. Competitive inhibitors compete with the substrate for binding at the active site. Other inhibitors bind elsewhere and alter enzyme activity.

5. Cofactors

Some enzymes require a non-protein component called a cofactor for catalytic activity. The main categories are:

  • Metal ions: Inorganic ions required by some enzymes.
  • Coenzymes: Small organic molecules that assist enzyme reactions.
  • Prosthetic groups: Non-protein components that are tightly associated with an enzyme.

A protein enzyme without its required cofactor is called an apoenzyme. The catalytically active combination of an apoenzyme and its required cofactor is called a holoenzyme.

6. Enzymes and specificity

Enzymes are often specific to particular substrates or types of reactions. For example, amylase acts on starch, while lipases act on lipids. This specificity helps cells carry out many reactions in a controlled manner.

7. Exam essentials

  • Enzymes are biological catalysts.
  • The substrate binds at the active site.
  • Enzymes lower activation energy.
  • Enzymes are not consumed by the overall reaction.
  • Temperature, pH, substrate concentration and inhibitors can affect activity.
  • Cofactors may be metal ions, coenzymes or prosthetic groups.
  • Apoenzyme plus its required cofactor forms the active holoenzyme.
  • Most enzymes are proteins, but some RNA molecules are catalytic.

Common exam traps

  • Do not confuse a nucleoside with a nucleotide: a nucleotide includes phosphate.
  • Do not confuse a peptide bond with a glycosidic bond.
  • Do not confuse the role of starch with that of cellulose: storage versus structure.
  • Do not assume every lipid is a true macromolecule.
  • Do not assume all enzymes are proteins; some RNA molecules are catalytic.
  • Do not say enzymes change the equilibrium position of a reaction.
  • Do not say denaturation normally breaks the primary amino-acid sequence.
  • Do not confuse DNA's thymine with RNA's uracil.
