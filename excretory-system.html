Excretory Products and Their Elimination

Explore how the human body removes metabolic wastes, how the kidneys filter blood, and how a tiny nephron helps maintain the body's internal balance.

01 · The big picture

Why do we need excretion?

Every living cell carries out metabolic activities. These activities produce waste substances. If wastes accumulate, they can disturb the normal functioning of cells and tissues.

Excretion is the process by which organisms eliminate metabolic wastes and other unwanted substances from the body.

Remember: Excretion removes metabolic wastes. Egestion is the removal of undigested food from the digestive tract. They are not the same process.

Excretion

Removal of metabolic wastes such as carbon dioxide, nitrogenous wastes and excess salts or water.

Osmoregulation

Maintenance of the water and solute balance of body fluids. Kidneys play a major role in this process.

02 · Nitrogenous wastes

Excretory products

Breakdown of proteins and nucleic acids produces nitrogenous wastes. Different animals eliminate nitrogen mainly in three forms.

Highly toxic

Ammonia

Very toxic and highly soluble in water. It needs a large amount of water for elimination.

Common in: Many aquatic animals.

Less toxic

Urea

Less toxic than ammonia. It is formed in the liver from ammonia and can be eliminated using less water.

Common in: Mammals, many terrestrial amphibians and cartilaginous fishes.

Least toxic

Uric acid

Least toxic and poorly soluble in water. It is excreted as a semi-solid paste, conserving water.

Common in: Birds, reptiles, insects and land snails.

Concept link: The form of nitrogenous waste is related to the animal's habitat and water availability. This is a broad biological pattern, not a rule that every member of a group must follow without exception.

04 · Human excretory system

The human excretory system

The human excretory system consists of a pair of kidneys, a pair of ureters, a urinary bladder and a urethra.

Kidneys

Bean-shaped, reddish-brown organs located in the abdominal cavity, one on either side of the vertebral column. They filter blood and form urine.

Ureters

Two narrow muscular tubes that carry urine from the kidneys to the urinary bladder.

Urinary bladder

A muscular, elastic sac that temporarily stores urine.

Urethra

A tube through which urine is released from the urinary bladder to the outside.

The inner concave side of each kidney has a notch called the hilum, through which the renal artery, renal vein, nerves and ureter are connected.
05 · Inside the kidney

External and internal structure of kidney

Two main regions

  • Renal cortex: The outer region of the kidney.
  • Renal medulla: The inner region, organised into cone-shaped masses called renal pyramids.

The cortex extends between the renal pyramids as renal columns of Bertini. The tips of the pyramids project into the calyces, which lead towards the renal pelvis.

06 · The functional unit

The nephron – the kidney's microscopic unit

Each kidney contains a very large number of nephrons (about one million). A nephron is the structural and functional unit of the kidney.

Two major components: (1) Glomerulus – a tuft of capillaries. (2) Renal tubule – begins with Bowman's capsule and continues through PCT, loop of Henle and DCT.

1. Glomerulus and Bowman's capsule

The glomerulus is a network of capillaries formed by the afferent arteriole and drained by the efferent arteriole. It is enclosed by a double-walled epithelial cup called Bowman's capsule.

The glomerulus and Bowman's capsule together form the Malpighian body (renal corpuscle).

2. Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)

The first highly coiled part of the renal tubule. Its epithelial cells have a brush border of microvilli, increasing the surface area for reabsorption.

3. Loop of Henle

A U-shaped part of the tubule with descending and ascending limbs. It dips into the medulla. The two limbs differ in their permeability to water and electrolytes.

4. Distal convoluted tubule (DCT)

The next coiled part of the tubule. It carries out selective reabsorption and secretion, helping regulate the composition of the filtrate.

5. Collecting duct

Receives fluid from nephrons and passes through the medulla towards the renal pelvis. It plays an important role in water reabsorption and urine concentration.

Types of nephrons

Cortical nephrons

Have their glomeruli in the outer cortex and relatively short loops of Henle.

Juxtamedullary nephrons

Have glomeruli closer to the medulla and long loops of Henle that extend deep into the medulla.

07 · How urine is formed

Formation of urine

Urine formation involves three main processes: glomerular filtration, selective reabsorption and tubular secretion.

Blood→Filtration→Reabsorption→Secretion→Urine

1. Glomerular filtration (ultrafiltration)

Blood is filtered through the three-layered filtration membrane: the fenestrated endothelium of glomerular blood vessels, the basement membrane, and the filtration slits between podocytes of Bowman's capsule.

Water and small solutes pass into Bowman's space. Blood cells and most large plasma proteins are normally retained in the blood.

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR)

About 125 mL of filtrate per minute in a healthy adult, or approximately 180 L per day.

Why so much filtrate?

Most of the filtrate is subsequently reabsorbed. Only a small fraction becomes final urine.

2. Selective reabsorption

Useful substances are taken back from the filtrate into the blood through the surrounding capillaries. These include glucose, amino acids, water and required ions. Reabsorption may occur by active or passive transport.

3. Tubular secretion

Some substances are transported from the blood into the tubular fluid. This helps remove additional wastes and regulate ionic and acid-base balance. Substances secreted include hydrogen ions, potassium ions and ammonia.

What happens in each part?

PartMain function
PCTReabsorbs nearly all essential nutrients and a major part of water and electrolytes; also helps maintain pH and ionic balance through secretion.
Descending limbPermeable to water; water moves out into the increasingly concentrated medullary interstitium.
Ascending limbImpermeable to water; electrolytes move out of the tubular fluid, helping establish the medullary osmotic gradient.
DCTConditional reabsorption of sodium and water, and reabsorption of bicarbonate; selective secretion of ions helps regulate pH and ionic balance.
Collecting ductReabsorbs water, helping concentrate urine; contributes to maintenance of pH and ionic balance.
Key distinction: Reabsorption moves substances from tubular fluid back into blood. Secretion moves substances from blood into tubular fluid.
08 · Concentrating urine

Counter-current mechanism

The loop of Henle and vasa recta help maintain an osmotic gradient in the renal medulla. This gradient allows water to be reabsorbed from the collecting duct and helps produce concentrated urine.

How does it work?

  1. Fluid flows in opposite directions in the descending and ascending limbs of the loop of Henle. This is called a counter-current arrangement.
  2. The descending limb is permeable to water, so water moves out into the medullary interstitium.
  3. The ascending limb is impermeable to water. Sodium chloride moves out, contributing to the osmotic gradient.
  4. Blood in the vasa recta also flows in opposite directions in its descending and ascending portions. This counter-current arrangement helps maintain the gradient.
  5. Urea from the collecting duct also contributes to the high osmolarity of the inner medulla.

Osmolarity increases from about 300 mOsmol/L in the cortex to about 1200 mOsmol/L in the inner medulla. This gradient helps water move out of the collecting duct, producing concentrated urine.

09 · Hormonal control

Regulation of kidney function

1. ADH (antidiuretic hormone)

ADH increases water reabsorption in the distal parts of the nephron and collecting duct. When body water is low, increased ADH helps conserve water and produces a smaller volume of more concentrated urine.

2. Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS)

A fall in glomerular blood flow, blood pressure or sodium chloride delivery can stimulate the juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA) to release renin. Renin initiates the conversion of angiotensinogen into angiotensin I; angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) converts it into angiotensin II.

Angiotensin II causes vasoconstriction and stimulates aldosterone release. Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption; water follows, helping restore blood volume and pressure.

3. Atrial natriuretic factor (ANF)

ANF is released by the atria of the heart when blood volume or pressure is high. It acts against the RAAS and promotes sodium and water loss, helping reduce blood pressure.

Juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA)

The JGA is a specialised region formed where the DCT comes in close contact with the afferent arteriole. It plays an important role in regulating GFR through the release of renin.

10 · From kidney to outside

Micturition and the nature of urine

Micturition

The process of releasing urine from the urinary bladder is called micturition. As the bladder fills, stretch receptors send signals to the central nervous system. A reflex is initiated, causing the bladder muscles to contract and the sphincter muscles to relax. The process is under nervous control and can be voluntarily regulated to some extent.

Normal urine

  • Usually pale yellow due to the presence of the pigment urochrome.
  • Slightly acidic on average, with a pH around 6; it can vary.
  • An adult typically produces about 1–1.5 litres per day, depending on fluid intake and other conditions.
  • Urine is approximately 95% water, 2.5% urea and 2.5% other dissolved substances, as described in NCERT.
11 · Clinical connections

Disorders of the excretory system

Uraemia

Abnormally high levels of urea in the blood, usually due to impaired kidney function.

Renal failure

A condition in which the kidneys lose their ability to perform normal functions adequately. Severe cases may require dialysis or kidney transplantation.

Renal calculi

Kidney stones formed by the crystallisation of certain salts in the urinary tract.

Glomerulonephritis

Inflammation of the glomeruli, which can interfere with normal filtration.

Haemodialysis

In haemodialysis, blood is passed through a dialyser containing a semipermeable membrane. Waste substances and excess salts move into the dialysis fluid, and the cleaned blood is returned to the body. Dialysis supports waste removal when kidneys cannot do so adequately; it does not perform every function of healthy kidneys.
